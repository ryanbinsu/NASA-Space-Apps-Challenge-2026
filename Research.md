SphereX Mission targets:
-More than 450 million galaxies: Maps the universe in 3D, scans the entire sky 4 times to create a comprehensive 3D map of 450 million+ galaxies.


-More than 100 million stars in the Milky Way: They are mostly used as background light to detect any icy interstellar clouds in between the stars and Earth.

-First near-infrared spectral all-sky survey- so it surveys the entire sky in different wavelengths - 102 infrared colour bands.

-Intended to explore the history of the universe - including the history of galaxies and, interestingly, water on planets.

-Allows direct measurement of dark matter distribution using data from SphereX alongside the Euclid and the Nancy Grace Roman Telescope, proposal was submitted in 2014.

Core scientific goals:
1. Understand why the universe is expanding and how it grew so large in a fraction of a second after the Big Bang. Done by measuring 3D positions and redshifts of hundreds of millions of galaxies, SphereX maps statistical patterns in how galaxies cluster. The patterns keep traces of any quantum fluctuations that happened during inflation, which help physicists rule out other theoretical models.
2. Measure the total amount of light galaxies have produced since the first stars formed. SphereX measures fluctuations in the collective infrared glow of the sky. It can help determine how galaxies formed and evolved.
3. Find where water and chemical building blocks of life are from, and how likely they are to be on other planets. Use absorption spectroscopy.



R Draconis:
About:
It is an old red giant star that is located in the  constellation Draco, it is also known as a Mira-variable pulsating star. The outer layers of the star begin to expand and contract by extreme anmounts every eight months, this changes the brightness of the star for a long period of time. The star ran out of the hydrogen and helium in its core, so now it is neutral oxygen and carbon, and fuses them in the shells around the core, which enlarges the star into a red giant. The surface temperature of the star is between 2400K-3700K, in comparison to the Sun being between 5,772-5,778K. The cool temperature gives the star a deep orange-red glow.

Strong stellar winds and pulsations will strip the outer gas layers of the star into space, this will form a glowing planetary nebula. The star will be left as a dense white dwarf core.
These kinds of stars have a pulsation period (information is for this specific star), taking around 245 days to complete one full contraction and expansion per cycle.
The brightness of R Draconis is not fixed due to the stellar winds, so we can try and assume the maximum brightness is ~7.5 and minimum being between 11.3 and 13.0. The brightness changes as Titanium oxide on the outer layer of the atmosphere blocks visible red light, so visual brightness decreases while infrared is more stable.
Mira variables are usually under two solar masses. R Draconis started out as a star larger than our Sun, however strong stellar winds have shed the outer layers of the star into space.
The luminosity is thousands of times more luminous than the Sun. It emits most of its energy as infrared radiation rather than visible light, the total energy output is thousands times brighter than the Sun.
By using Wien’s law and R Draconis’ cool temperatures, we can predict the peak energy output into the near-infrared. R Draconis is observed in infrared bands, for example: 2MASS, IRAS and WISE. They allow astronomers to capture the true bolometric luminosity. Luminosity depends on distance, but while distance may not be certain, we cannot accurately give the values for luminosity.

How the pulsation works briefly: Partial ionisation of hydrogen makes the layer more opaque, trapping heat. The surface that traps heat, and builds up pressure and radiation underneath the layer, which eventually means the pressure overcomes the gravitational forces, pushing the atmosphere of the star outwards, the radius of the star will increase. The gas will expand and cool, electrons will combine with atoms. When electrons combine, this makes the gas more transparent and therefore heat escapes causing a pressure drop. And the cycle will repeat.

Spectral types:
A spectral type is also known as a spectral classification, it is an astronomical system used to categorise stars based on surface temperature, chemical elements detected in their light and colour.

The temperature letters: O,B,A,F,G,K,M

O and B are extremely hot, massive blue-white stars with a range of 10,000 to over 30,000K.

A are hot white stars with a range 7,500-10,000K.

F and G are yellow-white to yellow stars. The sun is a G type star. 5,200-6,000K for G and 6,000-7,500K for F.

K are cooler orange stars with a temperature ranging from 3,700 to 5,200K.M are the coolest of stars, they glow red or a deep orange with temperatures of around under 3,700K.

Number subdivisions (0-9)

0 is defined as the hottest and 9 as the coolest, so M1 will be warmer than M8 at the M category.

Luminosity classes

Done in roman numerals, shows the size and brightness of the star.

I: supergiant

II:Bright giant

III:Regular giant

IV:Sub-giant

V: Main sequence stars

Special letters (for example “e”)

These are lowercase letters at the end, they show unusual things. So the “e” is for emission lines, so the star has a glowing gas around it. The bright emission lines in this case are formed from the superheated surrounding hydrogen gas. This is formed by the outward pulse sending shockwaves that crash into the thin upper atmosphere of the star, superheating hydrogen.

Epsilon Indi B: (Epoch J2000.0 ICRS)

A binary brown dwarf system that orbits the primary star Epsilon Indi A, containing Epsilon Indi Ba and Epsilon Indi Bb.
A binary brown dwarf system is where a pair of brown dwarfs orbit a common centre of mass (barycentre). Binary brown dwarfs are formed via gravitational collapse and fragmentation of a gas cloud. They do not undergo hydrogen fusion due to a lack of mass to generate extreme core temperatures and pressures needed to form helium from hydrogen. Because of this, they are very cool and dim, so don’t glow in visible light but mostly in infrared light. Brown dwarfs can be classified into different spectral types: M,L,T and Y.

M-type: 2,400-3,700K this is where the youngest and largest brown dwarfs start. The temperature is for M dwarfs in general, only the cool-end applies to brown dwarfs.

L-type: 1,300-2400K

T-type: 500-1,300K

Y-type: below 500K, only being visible in infrared light. They are the coldest known brown dwarfs.
The brown dwarfs can progress through all 4 spectral types.

Early estimates put the masses of Epsilon Indi Ba and Bb as 47 and 28 Jupiter masses before long-term orbital tracking. The total mass of the binary system is now about 120.2 Jupiter masses with Ba having a mass of 66.9 Jupiter masses (~6.4% the mass of the Sun), making it the larger brown dwarf. And Bb has a mass of 53.3 Jupiter masses (~5.1% of the mass of the Sun).
Epsilon Indi Ba is classified as a T1-1.5 and Bb classified as T6, as the numerical scale goes from 0-9, from hottest to coolest. Epsilon Indi B is one of the nearest binary brown dwarf systems to our solar system.

Epsilon Indi Ba has a luminosity of ~2x10^-5 relative to the Sun.

Epsilon Indi Bb has a luminosity of ~5.86x10^-6 relative to the Sun.

The dwarf system is invisible to the naked eye, however can be seen through near-infrared. While Epsilon Indi Ba outshines Bb as it makes up for about 77% the luminosity of the system. 

We can use the Stefan-Boltzmann law to estimate this as:

L= 4(pi)R^2 * T^4 * Stefan-Boltzmann constant

L= Luminosity (W)

4(pi)R^2= Surface area with R being the radius

T= Temperature (K) 

Stefan-Boltzmann constant= 5.67 x10^-8 (Wm^-2 K^-4)

-Both Ba and Bb have similar radii so the surface area will also be similar, Ba is roughly 1.4 times hotter than Bb, so (1.4^4), meaning Ba is almost 4x more luminous than Bb (the measured luminosity ratio is about 3.4x). And then we use the inverse-square law:

F= L/(4(pi)d^2)

F= Flux (Wm^-2). Apparent brightness

d= distance (m)

RW Aurigae A (RWAA) is the primary star with a spectral type between K1 and K3, and the mass is ~1.34 solar masses and surface temperature around 5,082K.
RW Aurigae B (RWAB) is the companion, being a smaller and cooler star with a spectral type K5 and mass ~0.9 solar masses. Despite its old age and slow rotation (approximately 114-130 days), occasionally produces stellar flares.

Stellar flares: most common in M-dwarfs. They are sudden power explosions of light, radiation and plasma on a star’s surface or atmosphere.


They can be caused by:
-Magnetic energy release: Stellar flares in stars that have outer convection zones.

-Magnetic reconnection: The star’s magnetic fields twist and tangle due to rotation and plasma motion.

-Conversion of energy: snapping converts stored magnetic energy into intense heat and radiation across entire electromagnetic spectrum from radio waves up to x-rays and gamma rays.

Barnard’s star is useful as it is the closest single star to Earth and an ancient red dwarf, making it good for testing exoplanet detection techniques around low-mass stars and studying the stellar flare.
